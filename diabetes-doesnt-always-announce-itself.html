Diabetes Doesn't Always Announce Itself

Why millions of people are living with diabetes without knowing it—and why early detection is about more than awareness.

What if you felt perfectly fine?

You wake up, go to work, run errands, meet friends and get on with your day. Nothing feels particularly wrong.

But what if, somewhere in the background, your blood glucose has been rising for years?

No dramatic warning. No obvious sign that something needs attention. For many people living with type 2 diabetes, this is how the condition can develop: quietly, gradually and sometimes without noticeable symptoms. By the time diabetes is diagnosed, complications may already have begun.

The absence of symptoms doesn't always mean the absence of disease.

Millions of people don't know they have diabetes

According to the International Diabetes Federation's 2025 Diabetes Atlas, an estimated 589 million adults aged 20–79 were living with diabetes worldwide in 2024. Of those, approximately 252 million—around 43%—were undiagnosed.

Behind those numbers are very different circumstances. Someone may have no symptoms and never think to request a test. Another person may recognise that something isn't right but struggle to find time away from work. Someone else may live hours from the nearest healthcare facility, where even a basic appointment comes with transport costs, lost income or uncertainty about what happens next.

These experiences are not interchangeable. Yet they can lead to the same outcome: diabetes remains undetected.

Why is diabetes so easy to miss?

Type 2 diabetes often develops over time. Some people notice increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, blurred vision or unexplained weight loss. Others experience symptoms so mild that they are easily attributed to stress, ageing or everyday exhaustion. Some experience no noticeable symptoms at all.

This is one reason why relying on symptoms alone is not enough to identify everyone living with diabetes.

Type 1 diabetes is different: it can develop rapidly and requires insulin treatment. Symptoms such as vomiting, abdominal pain and deep or rapid breathing can indicate a medical emergency and require urgent attention.

For people at increased risk of type 2 diabetes, appropriate testing can help identify the condition before symptoms or complications become apparent.

Where you live can influence whether you are diagnosed

A 2026 analysis based on the IDF Diabetes Atlas estimated that approximately 72.6% of adults living with diabetes in the IDF Africa Region were undiagnosed, compared with around 29.1% in North America and the Caribbean. These regional estimates mask substantial differences within countries and communities.

In one setting, a diabetes test may mean booking a routine appointment nearby. In another, it may mean arranging transport, missing a day's wages, paying for a consultation and hoping testing supplies are available.

Even after a diagnosis, people may face difficulties accessing medicines, follow-up appointments or reliable health information.

THE MHH PERSPECTIVE

Knowing that you should get tested is not the same as being able to get tested.

Diabetes awareness cannot be separated from healthcare access, affordability and inequality.

Early action: What does it actually mean?

The theme of World Diabetes Day 2026–2027 is Early Action on Diabetes.

Early action can mean understanding your individual risk, seeking medical advice when symptoms appear, receiving appropriate screening or diagnostic testing, and obtaining timely treatment and follow-up care. For people at increased risk of type 2 diabetes, early support can also help prevent or delay its development.

But early action shouldn't become another responsibility placed entirely on individuals. A person cannot make use of a screening service that doesn't exist. They cannot attend appointments they cannot afford. And a diagnosis alone does little if essential treatment remains out of reach.

What can you do?

If you have persistent thirst, frequent urination, unusual tiredness, blurred vision or unexplained weight loss, speak to a healthcare professional.

Even if you feel well, consider discussing your risk and whether testing is appropriate, particularly if you have a family history of diabetes, previously experienced gestational diabetes or have other recognised risk factors.

Depending on your circumstances, a healthcare professional may recommend HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose or another appropriate diagnostic test. Online risk questionnaires can help identify whether further assessment may be useful, but they cannot diagnose diabetes.

If symptoms develop rapidly, especially alongside vomiting, abdominal pain or deep or rapid breathing, seek urgent medical attention.

The question we should be asking

Every year on 14 November, World Diabetes Day brings renewed attention to a condition affecting hundreds of millions of people. Awareness campaigns matter—but awareness is only the beginning.

What happens after someone realises they might be at risk? Can they get tested? Can they afford the appointment? Will they receive understandable information? And if they're diagnosed, can they access the treatment and support they need?

The real goal isn't simply to help more people discover that they have diabetes. It's to make sure that knowing leads to care.
